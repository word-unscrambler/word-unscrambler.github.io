Why We Rebuilt the Engine

Our previous search engine served us well for years. It was a straightforward sorted-array approach: take your input letters, generate permutations, and cross-reference each against a flat word list. Simple, reliable, and sufficient — until it wasn't.

As our dictionary grew from 170,000 words to over 450,000 (encompassing TWL06, SOWPODS/Collins Scrabble Words, Words With Friends, and a specialized proper noun reference set), the permutation-based approach scaled poorly. A 7-letter rack generates 5,040 possible orderings. A rack with a blank tile effectively doubles that. On flagship desktop hardware, 45ms is barely perceptible. On a mid-range Android phone with a partially throttled CPU — the device used by the majority of our players during actual games — it translated to 180–300ms of lag per search.

300 milliseconds. In a Scrabble game where you might run 50+ searches per session, that's 15 seconds of cumulative waiting. Not acceptable.

Our goal was clear: sub-10ms on every device, every time.

The New Trie-Based Architecture

The new engine is built on a compressed trie (also called a DAWG — Directed Acyclic Word Graph) that pre-indexes the entire dictionary into a tree structure at build time, rather than resolving it at search time.

Green nodes mark valid word endings. The engine traverses only branches reachable with your available letters — impossible paths are pruned instantly without examination.

Here's why this is so much faster: the old permutation approach asks "is ABCDEFG a word? Is ABCDEGF a word? Is ABCDFEG a word?" — testing every arrangement. The trie approach asks "given my available letters, which paths through this tree can I reach?" Impossible branches are pruned the moment the engine determines no valid word prefix exists for a given letter combination.

For a 7-letter rack with no repeats, the old approach made up to 5,040 dictionary lookups. The new trie traversal averages fewer than 200 node visits to find every valid word — a 25× reduction in operations, before any hardware-level optimizations.

Bitwise Operations for Letter Counting

Checking whether a word's letter requirements can be satisfied by your rack is a frequent inner-loop operation. In the old engine, this was a character-by-character comparison using string manipulation. The new engine represents each rack state as a Uint8Array of 26 frequency counts (one per letter), and performs availability checks using bitwise comparisons — operations that execute in a single CPU cycle rather than the dozens required by string comparisons.

DAWG Compression

A raw trie for 450,000 words would be enormous — potentially hundreds of megabytes. A DAWG (Directed Acyclic Word Graph) solves this by merging identical suffixes across different words. The suffix "-ING" appears in tens of thousands of words; in a DAWG, all of them share the same three terminal nodes rather than each having their own copy. The result: our 450,000-word dictionary compresses to under 800KB — small enough to cache in a service worker and load in a single network roundtrip.

Offline Support via Service Workers

This was our most-requested feature from players who use word tools in venues with spotty connectivity — tournament halls, coffee shops, planes, basements. The new engine makes offline support technically possible because all computation happens client-side. We've paired it with a Service Worker that caches the dictionary DAWG file on first load.

After your first visit to the tool:

  1. The dictionary DAWG file is saved in your browser's cache storage (not localStorage — a persistent, quota-managed cache).
  2. On subsequent visits, the service worker serves the dictionary from cache, bypassing the network entirely.
  3. If you go offline mid-session, all searches continue functioning normally — the engine never contacts our servers.
  4. When a dictionary update is available, the service worker fetches it silently in the background and applies it on your next visit.

What's Coming Next

The trie infrastructure opens up several features that were previously too slow to implement reliably. We're actively working on:

  • Pattern matching: Enter a partial word with gaps (e.g., C?T) to find all words matching that pattern — ideal for crossword solvers.
  • Board-position filtering: Enter a letter already on the board as a fixed constraint so the engine only returns words that include that letter at a specific position.
  • Multi-language dictionaries: The DAWG format is language-agnostic. We're expanding to Spanish (FISE), French (ODS), and German (DSW) word lists in a future release.
  • Definition lookup: Hover-on-demand word definitions, sourced from our curated lexicographic database, to help you learn new words while you play.

A trie (rhymes with "try," from retrieval) is a tree-shaped data structure where each node represents a letter prefix shared by multiple words. The word "CAT", "CAB", "CARE", and "CAN" all share the prefix "CA" in the trie — they branch only at the third letter. This structure allows the engine to rapidly prune impossible search paths: if you don't have a "Q" in your rack, the engine instantly skips the entire Q-branch of the trie, which contains thousands of words. A DAWG (the variant we use) also merges identical suffixes, further shrinking the structure and reducing memory usage.
