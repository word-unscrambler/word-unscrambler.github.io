Brutalist Design: Why We Chose
High Contrast

In a digital landscape dominated by soft gradients, rounded corners, and pastel palettes, our site stands apart with sharp borders, stark contrast, and deliberate boldness. This wasn't an arbitrary aesthetic choice — it was a functional decision rooted in accessibility research, cognitive science, and a genuine belief that utility tools deserve to be honest about what they are.

What Is Brutalist Web Design?

The term "brutalism" in design doesn't mean harsh or unkind — it derives from the French béton brut, meaning "raw concrete," the defining material of the 1950s–1970s architectural movement. Brutalist architecture celebrated exposed structural elements: visible load-bearing walls, unfinished surfaces, massive geometric forms. Nothing was hidden. The building's honest nature was its aesthetic.

Translated to the web, brutalist design follows the same principle: raw, visible structure. Grid lines you can see. Borders that announce themselves. Typography that dominates rather than decorates. No faux-depth shadows trying to make flat interfaces look physical. No gradients pretending pixels have weight.

Neo-brutalism — the contemporary evolution we've adopted — adds a critical layer: it pairs that raw honesty with modern usability. The thick borders and bold contrast stay; the illegibility and intentional friction of "pure" brutalism go. The result is an interface that feels strong and confident without being hostile.

The Science of High Contrast

Human vision processes contrast before color, before shape, and before motion. The visual cortex performs a process called lateral inhibition — neurons at the edge of a high-contrast boundary fire more strongly than neurons in uniform regions, making edges the first thing we see. This is why black text on a white background is readable at a glance; it's why dark text on a pale-gray background requires the eye to work harder.

For a tool where you're scanning dozens of word results under time pressure — mid-game in Scrabble, solving a crossword before breakfast — this millisecond difference in visual processing compounds significantly.

The Accessibility Case

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 define three tiers of compliance: A, AA, and AAA. Most commercial websites target AA — a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text. We target AAA across our entire interface, which requires a 7:1 ratio for normal text.

This matters beyond checkbox compliance. Approximately 8% of men and 0.5% of women have some form of color vision deficiency. Over 246 million people worldwide have moderate-to-severe vision impairment. High contrast is not a niche accommodation — it's a design choice that directly improves the experience for hundreds of millions of people.

Cognitive Load Reduction

Cognitive load theory, developed by educational psychologist John Sweller, identifies three types of mental load: intrinsic (the inherent complexity of a task), extraneous (complexity imposed by poor design), and germane (mental effort that builds useful schemas). Good UI design's job is to minimize extraneous load so users can apply their full cognitive resources to the actual task.

For a word game tool, the intrinsic load is already high — you're evaluating dozens of possible words, checking board positions, and calculating scores simultaneously. Our job is to make the interface itself add zero friction. High-contrast design serves this goal in two specific ways:

  • Pre-attentive processing: High-contrast borders allow users to categorize page regions (input, filters, results) before consciously reading any labels. This happens in under 250 milliseconds — faster than conscious thought.
  • Reduced eye strain during extended sessions: A Scrabble tournament can last hours. Studies on display ergonomics consistently show that high-contrast text reduces eye fatigue compared to mid-contrast, low-saturation interfaces, particularly under fluctuating ambient light conditions.

Dark Mode: Not an Afterthought

Many websites treat dark mode as an inversion filter — simply flipping the light and dark values. The result is usually muddy: dark grays that lack depth, washed-out accents, and borders that disappear into the background.

Our design was conceived dark-first. The deep void background (#080b10) is not just dark — it has a subtle blue-shifted undertone that reduces the harsh blue-light component of white-on-dark interfaces while maintaining maximum perceived contrast. The neon cyan, green, and purple accents are calibrated for this specific background: their saturation and brightness are tuned to glow without blooming.

The light mode is a genuine re-design, not an inversion. Values shift to a warm-white base with desaturated navy accents, maintaining the same information hierarchy while using completely different chromatic logic appropriate for bright environments.

Typography: Mono, Sans, and Display

Typography in brutalist design is not neutral. Fonts carry personality, and in a tool that processes text — specifically, the letters of the alphabet as game pieces — font choices carry extra conceptual weight.

We use three typefaces, each with a distinct role:

  • Bebas Neue (Display): Used exclusively for the logo and large numerical displays. Its compressed, all-caps geometry echoes the tile-like quality of Scrabble letters — each character a discrete, geometric unit. It also loads in a single weight, keeping asset overhead minimal.
  • Plus Jakarta Sans (Body): A variable-weight geometric sans with genuine personality in its heavier weights. At 800 weight it's muscular and confident; at 400 it's clean and readable at body sizes. The slightly condensed proportions allow long words to display cleanly in results cards without truncation.
  • DM Mono (Labels & Code): Monospace typography for metadata, filter labels, and word results reinforces the "digital tool" character of the interface. Equal-width characters also make scanning lists of words easier — your eye travels in a predictable rhythm.

Performance as a Design Value

High-contrast, brutalist design has an underappreciated performance dividend: it relies heavily on CSS rather than images. Thick borders are border: 3px solid — zero network requests. Bold shadows are CSS box-shadow — GPU-composited, cheap to render. The entire visual language of the site costs almost nothing in bytes.

This aligns directly with our engineering philosophy. Our new word processing engine was built for speed — achieving sub-10ms search times by running entirely client-side. The design matches: a fast interface that respects your time and your data plan.

Performance Stats

Our Lighthouse performance score sits at 98/100. The design's CSS-centric approach contributes significantly — no hero image carousels, no heavy font stacks, no decorative gradients requiring large image assets.
